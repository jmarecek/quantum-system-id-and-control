\chapter{Sample Complexity of Identification}\label{C.SampleComplexity}

\begin{frame}\ft{Outline}
\ona{\Chaps~\ref{C.StateSpace} and~\ref{C.Frequency} answered a structural question: given exact expectation values, when do the data determine the model? The answer was a set of rank conditions, and the last of them, in \Chap~\ref{C.Frequency}, said that a mode reaches the data only if the experiment excites it, the measurement sees it, and the sampling keeps it apart from the others. But an experiment never returns an expectation value. It returns one outcome of one measurement on one copy of the state, and the copy is destroyed in the process. Every number we have used so far, from the coherence vector to the Hankel matrix, is in practice an average over a finite number of such \emph{shots}, and carries an error. In this \chap{} we ask how many shots are needed for a prescribed accuracy, which is the \emph{sample complexity} of the problem. We shall find that the answer has two factors. One, proportional to $1/\eps^2$ for an accuracy $\eps$, is the price of probabilistic measurement and cannot be avoided with independent shots. The other measures how weakly the experiment excites and sees the system: the rank conditions of the previous \chaps{} become quantitative, and a system that is only just identifiable is very expensive to identify.}
\onp{Outline of today's lecture:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.35em}
    \item identifiable, but at what cost?
    \item shots: a measurement is a coin toss; the $1/\sqrt N$ law and Hoeffding's inequality;
    \item Fisher information and the Cram\'er--Rao bound; when to measure; the error ellipse;
    \item quantum limits: the best measurement, and time as a resource (the Heisenberg limit);
    \item the cost of tomography, and classical shadows;
    \item learning a Hamiltonian from its Gibbs state: copies, time and bits;
    \item Ho--Kalman on quantum data; weakly excited, weakly seen, nearly merged;
    \item an error budget for the identification of a master equation.
\end{itemize}}

\section{Shots}\label{sec:ch06:shots}

\begin{frame}\ft{A measurement is a coin toss}
\ona{Let $O$ be an observable with eigenvalues $\pm1$, such as a Pauli operator, and let $\mu=\Tr[O\rho]$ be its expectation value. A single measurement of $O$ on one copy of $\rho$ yields $+1$ with probability $\frac12(1+\mu)$ and $-1$ with probability $\frac12(1-\mu)$, so that it is in effect the toss of a biased coin. Its mean is $\mu$ and its variance is $1-\mu^2$. Measuring $N$ fresh copies gives independent outcomes $o_1,\dots,o_N$, and their mean $\hat\mu=\frac1N\sum_io_i$ is the natural estimate of $\mu$. It has mean $\mu$, so that it is unbiased, and variance $(1-\mu^2)/N$, so that its typical error is $\sqrt{(1-\mu^2)/N}$. The error falls like $1/\sqrt N$: to halve it one must quadruple the number of shots. Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:coins}(a) shows the running mean of several sequences of outcomes settling into the band $\mu\pm\sqrt{(1-\mu^2)/N}$, and Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:coins}(b) the distribution of $\hat\mu$, which narrows by a factor of $\sqrt{10}$ for every tenfold increase in $N$. Note that the variance is smallest when $\mu$ is close to $\pm1$, that is, when the state is close to an eigenstate of $O$, and largest when $\mu=0$.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item One shot: $\pm1$ with probabilities $\frac12(1\pm\mu)$; mean $\mu$, variance $1-\mu^2$.
    \item $N$ shots: $\hat\mu=\frac1N\sum o_i$ has variance $(1-\mu^2)/N$; the error falls like \alert{$1/\sqrt N$}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}\ft{How many shots: Hoeffding's inequality}
\ona{The variance tells us the typical error, but we often want a guarantee: that the error is at most $\eps$ except with a small probability $\delta$. Such guarantees are given by concentration inequalities, of which the following is the simplest.}
\begin{thm}[Hoeffding]\label{thm:ch06:hoeffding}
For independent $o_i\in[-1,1]$ with mean $\mu$, $\Prob\big(\abs{\hat\mu-\mu}\ge\eps\big)\le2\exp(-N\eps^2/2)$. Hence $N\ge\frac{2}{\eps^2}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}$ shots ensure $\abs{\hat\mu-\mu}<\eps$ with probability at least $1-\delta$.
\end{thm}
\ona{The bound is due to \citet{Hoeffding1963}. It does not use the variance, and so it is pessimistic when $\mu$ is close to $\pm1$, but it has the right shape (Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:hoeffding}(a)). Two features of the shot count deserve emphasis. The dependence on the accuracy is $1/\eps^2$: ten times the accuracy costs a hundred times the shots (Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:hoeffding}(b)). The dependence on the confidence is only logarithmic, which has a useful consequence. If $M$ expectation values are to be estimated, each from its own $N$ shots, the probability that any one of them is in error by $\eps$ or more is at most $M$ times the probability for one of them, so that $N\ge\frac{2}{\eps^2}\ln\frac{2M}{\delta}$ shots each suffice for all of them at once. Many expectation values cost little more per value than one.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item $N\ge\frac{2}{\eps^2}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}$: accuracy is expensive ($\eps^{-2}$), confidence is cheap ($\ln\frac1\delta$).
    \item $M$ expectation values: $N\ge\frac{2}{\eps^2}\ln\frac{2M}{\delta}$ each.
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\section{How much a shot can tell}\label{sec:ch06:fisher}

\begin{frame}\ft{When to measure}
\ona{Most of the quantities we wish to learn, such as a frequency or a decay rate, are not expectation values but parameters on which the expectation values depend. How much a single shot tells us about such a parameter $\theta$ depends on how strongly the probabilities of its outcomes depend on $\theta$. Suppose that we measure $\sigma_x$ at time $t$, so that $\mu(t;\theta)=\langle\sigma_x\rangle(t)$. If a small change $\delta\theta$ changes $\mu$ by $\partial_\theta\mu\,\delta\theta$, the change can be detected with $N$ shots when it exceeds their typical error $\sqrt{(1-\mu^2)/N}$. The ratio of the squares of the two,}
\onp{Information about $\theta$ in one shot of a $\pm1$ observable with mean $\mu(\theta)$:}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch06:fisherbinary}
    \CFI(\theta)=\frac{(\partial_\theta\mu)^2}{1-\mu^2},
\end{equation}
\ona{is the information that one shot carries about $\theta$; it is the Fisher information of the shot, defined in general below. Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:fisher} shows it as a function of the time of measurement, for the frequency $\omega$ and the decay rate $\Gamma$ of our qubit. The frequency is best measured where the curve $\langle\sigma_x\rangle(t)$ crosses zero, since there a change of $\omega$ moves it most, and the decay rate near its extremes. Both are subject to the envelope $t^2\ee^{-2\Gamma t}$, which is greatest at $t=1/\Gamma$: too early, and the phase $\omega t$ has had no time to depend appreciably on $\omega$; too late, and the signal has decayed. The relaxation rate $\gamma_1$, measured through $\sigma_z$, offers a small surprise. The sensitivity $\partial_{\gamma_1}\langle\sigma_z\rangle$ is greatest at $t=1/\gamma_1=5$, but the information is greatest later, near $t\approx8.7$. The reason is the denominator in \eqref{eq:ch06:fisherbinary}: as $\langle\sigma_z\rangle$ approaches $1$ the state approaches an eigenstate of $\sigma_z$, the noise of the shots dies away, and a small change is more easily seen. A well-designed experiment spends its shots at such times.}
\onp{\begin{center}\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{\input{figures/ch06_fisher.tex}}\end{center}}

\resizebox{\linewidth}{!}{\input{figures/ch06_fisher.tex}}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}\ft{Fisher information and the Cram\'er--Rao bound}
\ona{The idea extends to any number of parameters and any kind of measurement. Let the outcome $o$ of a shot have the probability $p_\theta(o)$, depending on parameters $\theta\in\R^m$.}
\begin{defn}[Fisher information]\label{def:ch06:fisher}
The Fisher information of one shot is the $m\times m$ matrix $\CFI(\theta)_{ab}=\Expect_\theta\big[\partial_a\log p_\theta(o)\,\partial_b\log p_\theta(o)\big]$.
\end{defn}
\ona{For a $\pm1$ outcome with mean $\mu(\theta)$ the definition gives $[\CFI]_{ab}=\partial_a\mu\,\partial_b\mu/(1-\mu^2)$, which is \eqref{eq:ch06:fisherbinary}. Fisher information is additive over independent shots: $N$ shots at each of the times $t_1,\dots,t_K$ carry $N\sum_k[\CFI]_k(\theta)$. Its importance lies in the following theorem, due to Cram\'er and Rao in the 1940s.}
\begin{thm}[Cram\'er--Rao]\label{thm:ch06:cramerrao}
Every unbiased estimator $\hat\theta$ computed from data with Fisher information $\CFI$ has covariance $\Cov(\hat\theta)\succeq\CFI(\theta)^{-1}$. The maximum-likelihood estimator attains the bound asymptotically, as the number of shots grows.
\end{thm}
\ona{Thus no method whatever can estimate $\theta$ from $N$ independent shots with an error smaller than of order $1/\sqrt{N\CFI}$, and a good method attains this. Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:shots} compares the bound with the errors of a least-squares fit of $\gamma_1$ to data of our qubit; the two agree closely for all but the smallest numbers of shots, so that least squares is here nearly as good as possible. The bound also explains the $1/\sqrt N$ law of the previous section. It is not a defect of the estimator but a property of independent shots, and it can be escaped only by measurements that are not independent, which we take up in Section~\ref{sec:ch06:quantum}.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item Additive over shots: $N$ shots at $K$ times carry $N\sum_k[\CFI]_k$.
    \item No unbiased estimator does better than $\CFI^{-1}$; maximum likelihood attains it asymptotically.
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}\ft{Several parameters: the error ellipse}
\ona{When several parameters are estimated together, the inverse Fisher matrix describes an ellipse in parameter space, $\{\theta:(\theta-\hat\theta)^\top\CFI(\theta-\hat\theta)\le1\}$, within which the estimates of repeated experiments fall about as often as a Gaussian variable falls within one standard deviation. Its axes are the eigenvectors of $\CFI$, and their lengths are the reciprocals of the square roots of its eigenvalues. Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:ellipse} shows the ellipse for the rates $(\gamma_1,\gamma_\phi)$ of our qubit, together with the estimates from repeated simulated experiments, which fill it as the theorem says they should. For the preparation at $60^\circ$ the standard deviations are $0.003$ for $\gamma_1$ and $0.025$ for $\gamma_\phi$; at $15^\circ$, where the precession is weakly excited, that of $\gamma_\phi$ grows to $0.083$, while that of $\gamma_1$ hardly changes. The ellipse has a simple geometric meaning. Near its minimum, the least-squares cost of fitting the parameters to the data is approximately a quadratic form whose matrix is, up to a factor, the Fisher matrix, so that the ellipse is the shape of the bottom of the valley of the cost. When a direction in parameter space cannot be identified at all, the valley is flat along it, as in Figure~\ref{fig:ch04:valley} of \Chap~\ref{C.StateSpace}: the Fisher matrix is singular, and the ellipse is infinitely long. Structural identifiability is the statement that $\CFI$ is invertible; its practical counterpart is that $\CFI$ is well conditioned.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item Error ellipse $(\theta-\hat\theta)^\top\CFI(\theta-\hat\theta)\le1$: the bottom of the least-squares valley.
    \item Unidentifiable direction $\Leftrightarrow$ singular $\CFI$; poorly identifiable $\Leftrightarrow$ ill-conditioned $\CFI$.
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}\ft{Weakly excited means expensive}
\ona{We can now account for Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:cost}. By \eqref{eq:ch05:sum} of \Chap~\ref{C.Frequency}, the data are a sum of modes $r_i\ee^{\lambda_it}$ with weights $r_i=(\mathbf C\vec v_i)(\vec w_i^\dagger\vec x_0)$, the product of what the measurement sees of the mode and of how much the preparation excites it. The parameters of a mode enter the data only through that mode, and the derivative of $r_i\ee^{\lambda_it}$ with respect to $\lambda_i$ is $r_it\,\ee^{\lambda_it}$, proportional to $r_i$. By \eqref{eq:ch06:fisherbinary}, the Fisher information about $\lambda_i$ is therefore proportional to $\abs{r_i}^2$, and the number of shots needed for a given accuracy to $1/\abs{r_i}^2$:}
\onp{Weight of a mode $r_i=(\mathbf C\vec v_i)(\vec w_i^\dagger\vec x_0)$ (\Chap~\ref{C.Frequency}); then}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch06:weight}
    \CFI(\lambda_i)\;\propto\;\abs{r_i}^2=\abs{\mathbf C\vec v_i}^2\,\abs{\vec w_i^\dagger\vec x_0}^2,\qquad
    N\;\propto\;\frac{1}{\abs{\mathbf C\vec v_i}^2\,\abs{\vec w_i^\dagger\vec x_0}^2\,\eps^2}.
\end{equation}
\ona{For the qubit prepared at the angle $\phi$, the precession is excited with $\abs{\vec w^\dagger\vec x_0}\propto\sin\phi$, and the cost grows like $1/\sin^2\phi$. In the same way a mode that is weakly seen, with $\mathbf C\vec v_i$ small, is expensive. Persistency of excitation and observability, which in \Chaps~\ref{C.StateSpace} and~\ref{C.Frequency} were conditions to be satisfied or not, have become quantities: the cost of an experiment is inversely proportional to the square of how well it excites and sees each mode.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item Cost of a mode $\propto1/\abs{r_i}^2$: weakly excited or weakly seen means expensive.
    \item Persistency of excitation and observability become \alert{quantities}.
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\section{Quantum limits}\label{sec:ch06:quantum}

\begin{frame}\ft{The best measurement: quantum Fisher information}
\ona{The Fisher information depends on the measurement. In quantum mechanics we may choose the measurement, and it is natural to ask which choice gives the most information. Let the state $\rho_\theta$ depend on $\theta$, and let the measurement be a POVM $\{E_o\}$, so that $p_\theta(o)=\Tr[E_o\rho_\theta]$.}
\begin{defn}[Quantum Fisher information]\label{def:ch06:qfi}
For one parameter, the quantum Fisher information is $\QFI(\theta)=\Tr[\rho_\theta L_\theta^2]$, where the symmetric logarithmic derivative $L_\theta$ is defined by $\partial_\theta\rho_\theta=\frac12\acomm{L_\theta}{\rho_\theta}$.
\end{defn}
\begin{thm}[Quantum Cram\'er--Rao]\label{thm:ch06:qcr}
For every POVM, $\CFI(\theta)\le\QFI(\theta)$, with equality for the measurement in the eigenbasis of $L_\theta$. Hence every unbiased estimator from $N$ shots has $\Var(\hat\theta)\ge1/(N\QFI(\theta))$ \citep{HOLEVO_2019}.
\end{thm}
\ona{For a qubit with Bloch vector $\vec r_\theta$ the quantities are explicit. A measurement of the Pauli operator along the unit vector $\vec m$ has $\CFI=(\vec m\cdot\partial_\theta\vec r)^2/(1-(\vec m\cdot\vec r)^2)$, and $\QFI=\abs{\partial_\theta\vec r}^2+(\vec r\cdot\partial_\theta\vec r)^2/(1-\abs{\vec r}^2)$. For a vector that rotates, as in a measurement of $\omega$, the velocity is perpendicular to $\vec r$, and the best measurement is along the velocity, perpendicular to the Bloch vector in the plane of motion (Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:qfi}). For a pure state, $\ket{\psi_\theta}=\ee^{-\imath\theta H}\ket\psi$, the quantum Fisher information is $4\Var_\psi(H)$. The quantum Fisher information is also a Riemannian metric on the set of states, the Bures metric of \Chap~\ref{C.GeometryStates} \citep{Braunstein1994}: how well a parameter can be estimated and how far apart the neighbouring states lie are one and the same geometric quantity.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item $\CFI\le\QFI$ for every measurement; equality in the eigenbasis of $L_\theta$.
    \item Qubit: measure perpendicular to the Bloch vector, in the plane of motion. Pure states: $\QFI=4\Var_\psi(H)$.
    \item $\QFI$ is the Bures metric (\Chap~\ref{C.GeometryStates}).
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}\ft{Time as a resource: the Heisenberg limit}
\ona{So far the shots were independent and identically prepared, and the error fell like $1/\sqrt N$. Time is a resource of a different kind. After an evolution for the time $t$ under $\ee^{-\imath\omega tH}$, the phase is proportional to $\omega t$, the quantum Fisher information about $\omega$ is $4t^2\Var_\psi(H)$, and the error from a single shot is proportional to $1/t$. If the total time available is $T$, the error of $T/t$ repetitions at a fixed short time $t$ falls only like $1/\sqrt T$, but a single long evolution would give $1/T$. The difficulty is that a long evolution determines the phase $\omega t$ only modulo $2\pi$, which is the aliasing of \Chap~\ref{C.Frequency} once more. Robust phase estimation \citep{Kimmel2015robust} overcomes it by a sequence of evolutions of doubling length, each of which selects one of the candidates allowed by the previous one, as with the combs of the multirate condition (Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:heisenberg}(a)). The error then falls like $1/T$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:heisenberg}(b)), which is the \emph{Heisenberg limit} \citep{Giovannetti2006}, the best possible scaling.}
\ona{For an open system the advantage is limited. The information gained in an evolution of length $t$ is reduced by the factor $\ee^{-2\Gamma t}$ through decoherence, so that it is best to repeat evolutions of length about $1/(2\Gamma)$, and once $T$ exceeds this the error falls once again like $1/\sqrt T$. For many-body Hamiltonians $H=\sum_\alpha\theta_\alpha P_\alpha$ with $p$ local terms, \citet{Huang2023heisenberg} interleave the evolution with random local operations that isolate one term at a time, and learn all $p$ coefficients to accuracy $\eps$ with total evolution time of order $p/\eps$, up to logarithmic factors. Without such control, learning from the natural dynamics with product preparations and local measurements obeys the $1/\eps^2$ law \citep{Bairey2019}, which is the regime of the methods of \Chaps~\ref{C.StateSpace} and~\ref{C.Frequency}.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item Evolution for time $t$: $\QFI\propto t^2$, but the phase is known only modulo $2\pi$ (aliasing).
    \item Robust phase estimation: doubling times, one tooth at a time; error $\propto1/T$, the \alert{Heisenberg limit}.
    \item Decoherence at rate $\Gamma$ caps the gain: beyond $T\approx1/\Gamma$, back to $1/\sqrt T$.
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\section{Tomography and classical shadows}\label{sec:ch06:tomography}

\begin{frame}\ft{The cost of tomography}
\ona{Quantum state tomography (\Chap~\ref{C.AlgTomography}) estimates the whole density operator $\rho$ on $\C^d$, which has $d^2-1$ real parameters. The elementary method measures each of the $d^2-1$ Pauli observables separately and assembles $\hat\rho$ from the estimates. By Theorem~\ref{thm:ch06:hoeffding}, with $N/(d^2-1)$ shots each, every coefficient is in error by about $d/\sqrt N$. The error matrix $\hat\rho-\rho$ then has a Frobenius norm of order $\sqrt{d^3/N}$, and its trace norm is at most $\sqrt d$ times as large, so that the trace distance is of order $d^2/\sqrt N$, and $N$ of order $d^4/\eps^2$ copies suffice for accuracy $\eps$. Better use of single copies reduces this to order $d^3/\eps^2$, and measurements on many copies at once reduce it further.}
\begin{thm}[Sample complexity of tomography; \citealp{haah2016sample,ODonnell2016efficient}]\label{thm:ch06:tomography}
There is a measurement on $N=\bigO{rd/\eps^2}$ copies of a state of rank $r$ which returns $\hat\rho$ with $\tracenorm{\hat\rho-\rho}\le\eps$ with high probability, and no procedure succeeds with substantially fewer copies. With measurements on single copies, $\bigOmega{d^3/\eps^2}$ copies are necessary for states of full rank.
\end{thm}
\ona{The lower bound is at heart a count of parameters: $d^2-1$ numbers, each to accuracy $\eps$, cannot be learnt from much fewer than $d^2/\eps^2$ copies, since each copy carries a bounded amount of information. For $q$ qubits, $d=2^q$, and the cost of full tomography grows exponentially with $q$ whichever method is used (Figure~\ref{fig:ch01:tomography_scaling} of \Chap~\ref{C.Motivation}). The identification methods of \Chaps~\ref{C.StateSpace} and~\ref{C.Frequency}, which take the coherence vector as their input, inherit this cost when they require it in full. The way out is to ask for less, or to use structure: locality, sparsity, or low rank.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item Single-copy measurements: $\bigTheta{d^3/\eps^2}$; joint measurements: $\bigTheta{d^2/\eps^2}$ for full rank \citep{haah2016sample,ODonnell2016efficient}.
    \item $d=2^q$: exponential in the number of qubits; the escape is \alert{structure}, or asking for less.
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}\ft{Classical shadows}
\ona{Often we need not $\rho$ itself but only the expectation values of $M$ observables $O_1,\dots,O_M$, for instance of the $p$ measured observables at each of the times that enter the Hankel matrix. The classical-shadow protocol of \citet{Huang2020} applies to each copy a random unitary $U$ from a fixed ensemble, such as a random Pauli rotation of every qubit, measures in the computational basis, and records the outcome $\ket b$. From $U$ and $b$ it forms the \emph{classical shadow} $\hat\rho=\mathcal M^{-1}(U^\dagger\ket b\bra bU)$, where $\mathcal M$ is the known average effect of the random measurement. Each shadow is an unbiased estimator of $\rho$, so that $\Tr[O_k\hat\rho]$ is an unbiased estimator of $\Tr[O_k\rho]$ for every $k$ at once.}
\begin{thm}[\citealp{Huang2020}]\label{thm:ch06:shadows}
With median-of-means post-processing,
\[
    N=\bigO{\frac{\log(M/\delta)}{\eps^2}\,\max_k\norm{O_k}_{\mathrm{shadow}}^2}
\]
shadows suffice to estimate all $M$ expectation values to accuracy $\eps$ with probability $1-\delta$. For random Pauli measurements and an observable $O_k$ acting on $w$ qubits, $\norm{O_k}^2_{\mathrm{shadow}}\le4^w\norm{O_k}_\infty^2$, and for a Pauli string of weight $w$ the variance of one shadow is $3^w-\langle O_k\rangle^2$.
\end{thm}
\ona{The number of copies grows only logarithmically with the number of observables, and does not depend on the number of qubits at all for observables of fixed weight (Figures~\ref{fig:ch06:snapshots} and~\ref{fig:ch06:shadows}). This is what the identification of a local model needs: local Pauli expectation values at many times, all drawn from one pool of shots. The dependence on the accuracy remains $1/\eps^2$.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item Random rotation and computational-basis measurement: an unbiased estimator of $\rho$, never stored as a matrix.
    \item $\bigO{3^w\log M/\eps^2}$ copies for $M$ Pauli observables of weight $w$: \alert{independent of the number of qubits}.
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\section{Learning a Hamiltonian from its Gibbs state}\label{sec:ch06:gibbs}

\begin{frame}\ft{A state in equilibrium}
\ona{So far the data have come from dynamics: a state prepared, left to evolve, and measured at many times. There is a second, older way of learning a Hamiltonian, which needs no clock at all. A system left long enough in contact with a bath at the inverse temperature $\beta$ comes to the Gibbs state}
\onp{Copies of the Gibbs state at a known inverse temperature $\beta$:}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch06:gibbs}
    \rho=\frac{\ee^{-\beta H}}{\Tr\ee^{-\beta H}},\qquad H=\sum_{a=1}^m\lambda_aE_a,\quad\abs{\lambda_a}\le1,
\end{equation}
\ona{where each $E_a$ is a product of Pauli matrices acting non-trivially on at most $k$ qubits, so that $H$ is \emph{$k$-local}. Given copies of $\rho$ and the value of $\beta$, we are to estimate the coefficients $\lambda_a$. The \emph{dual interaction graph} of $H$ has the terms $a=1,\dots,m$ as its vertices, with an edge between $a$ and $b$ whenever $E_a$ and $E_b$ act on a common qubit; for the lattice models of physics its degree is bounded. Two features of \eqref{eq:ch06:gibbs} govern everything that follows. First, $\rho$ does not change when a multiple of the identity is added to $H$, so that only differences of energies can be learnt. Second, the temperature does the work that the preparation did in the earlier sections: at a high temperature every level is populated but $\rho$ differs little from $\Id/d$, while at a low temperature only the ground state is populated, and the part of $H$ that acts on the excited levels is not excited at all.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item $H$ is $k$-local; the \alert{dual interaction graph} joins terms that share a qubit.
    \item Only differences of energies can be learnt; the \alert{temperature plays the part of the preparation}.
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}\ft{Temperature as excitation}
\ona{Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:gibbs} makes the point for the qubit of \Chap~\ref{C.Frequency} and for the qubit with its defect from \Chap~\ref{C.StateSpace}. Since the eigenvectors of $H$ do not depend on the parameter in either case, the quantum Fisher information is $\beta^2$ times the variance of $\partial E/\partial\theta$ under the populations, and it is attained by measuring in the basis of energies. At high temperature the state differs from $\Id/d$ only in the first order of $\beta$, each copy tells us about $H$ only to that order, and the number of copies grows like $1/\beta^2$, the rate of \citet{haah2022optimal}. At low temperature the state is nearly the ground state, and the ground state $\ket{00}$ of the pair does not depend on $g$ at all. What information remains comes from the first excited level, whose population is $\ee^{-\beta\Delta}$, and the cost grows like $\ee^{\beta\Delta}$. Between the two lies a best temperature. A level that is not populated is a mode that is not excited.}

\begin{frame}\ft{Copies and time}
\ona{The number of copies needed was shown to be polynomial in $m$ by \citet{anshu2021sample}, without an efficient algorithm. At high temperature, below a constant $\beta_c$ that depends on the locality, \citet{haah2022optimal} gave an efficient algorithm that uses $\bigO{\log(n/\delta)/(\beta^2\eps^2)}$ copies, and showed this number to be optimal; the factor $1/\beta^2$ comes from a state that is nearly maximally mixed. \citet{bakshi2023learning} removed the restriction on the temperature, at the cost of an analysis by polynomial optimisation, and \citet{wodecki2024learning} carried it through with Chebyshev polynomials in place of Taylor's, together with an account of the number of bits. Their result reads as follows.}
\begin{thm}[\citealp{wodecki2024learning}]\label{thm:ch06:gibbs}
Let $H$ be a $k$-local Hamiltonian on $n$ qubits with $m$ terms, whose dual interaction graph $G$ has bounded degree, and let $\beta$ be known. For every $\eps,\delta>0$ there is an algorithm which, with probability at least $1-\delta$, returns $\hat\lambda$ with $\abs{\hat\lambda_a-\lambda_a}\le\eps$ for all $a$, using
\[
    \bigO{\frac{m^6}{\eps^{\,\exp(C(k,G)\beta)}}\,\ln\frac m\delta}
\]
copies of $\rho$. If the numbers it computes have $\mathrm{poly}(n,\log(1/\eps))$ bits, its running time is
\[
    \bigl[m+m^{\bigO{1}}\eps^{-\exp(C(k,G)\beta)}\bigr]^{E},\qquad E\le2^{C(k,G)\beta+1}\bigl(\ee+\ln(1/\eps)\bigr),
\]
where the exponent $E$ does not depend on $n$.
\end{thm}
\ona{The bound on copies is polynomial in $m$ for every fixed temperature, but the exponent of $1/\eps$ grows very fast with $\beta$. The single qubit of Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:gibbs} shows why some growth is unavoidable: at a low temperature the information about the excited levels arrives only with their population, which is exponentially small in $\beta$.}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}\ft{Direction and scale}
\ona{The algorithms of \citet{bakshi2023learning} and \citet{wodecki2024learning} search for a Hamiltonian $\hat H=\sum_a\hat\lambda_aE_a$ that satisfies two families of constraints, each with a small tolerance and each evaluated on estimates of local expectation values. The first is stationarity: the Gibbs state commutes with its Hamiltonian, so that $\Tr(A[\hat H,\rho])\approx0$ for local observables $A$. These constraints are linear in $\hat\lambda$, but they cannot fix the scale, since every multiple of $H$ commutes with $\rho$. The second is the condition of Kubo, Martin and Schwinger,
\[
    \Tr\bigl(B_2\,\ee^{-\beta\hat H}B_1\ee^{\beta\hat H}\rho\bigr)=\Tr(B_1B_2\rho),
\]
which holds at the known temperature for $H$ and for no other traceless Hamiltonian. The conjugation $\ee^{X}B_1\ee^{-X}$ is the exponential of the map $B\mapsto[X,B]$; replacing the exponential by a polynomial $Q$ gives the sum $Q(X\,|\,B_1)=\sum_iq_i[X,B_1]_i$ of nested commutators, and the constraint becomes polynomial in $\hat\lambda$. Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:direction} shows the two sets for the driven qubit of \Chap~\ref{C.StateSpace}. Stationarity confines $(\hat\omega,\hat u)$ to a band through the origin, which fixes the direction only; the second family alone already fixes both direction and scale, and the band merely trims it. Both shrink like $N^{-1/2}$.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item Stationarity: $\Tr(A[\hat H,\rho])\approx0$, linear in $\hat\lambda$; fixes the \alert{direction} only.
    \item KMS: $\Tr(B_2\,Q(-\beta\hat H\,|\,B_1)\,\rho)\approx\Tr(B_1B_2\rho)$, $Q\approx\exp$; fixes the \alert{scale} as well.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}\ft{A flat approximation of the exponential}
\ona{The polynomial $Q$ must be accurate where the spectrum of $-\beta H$ lies, and it must not be large elsewhere, since the moment/SOS relaxation works with pseudo-distributions that are not confined to the spectrum. \citet{bakshi2023learning} made this precise.}
\begin{defn}[Flat approximation; \citealp{bakshi2023learning}]\label{def:ch06:flat}
Let $\eps,\eta\in(0,1)$ and $K>0$. A polynomial $p$ is an $(\eps,\eta,K)$-flat approximation of the exponential if $\abs{p(x)-\ee^x}\le\eps$ for $x\in[-K,K]$, and $\abs{p(x)}\le\max(1,\ee^x)\,\ee^{\eta\abs x}$ for every real $x$.
\end{defn}
\ona{A truncated series fails the second condition: a polynomial of degree $D$ that is accurate on an interval grows like $\abs x^D/D!$ outside it. Bakshi and his co-authors multiplied truncated Taylor series; \citet{wodecki2024learning} multiply $k$ Chebyshev truncations $f_n$ of $\ee^{x/k}$, of degrees $2l,4l,\dots,2^kl$,
\[
    Q_{k,l}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^kf_{2^il}\Bigl(\frac xk\Bigr),\qquad f_n(y)=I_0(t)+2\sum_{j=1}^nI_j(t)\,T_j(y/t),
\]
with $I_j$ the modified Bessel functions and $T_j$ the Chebyshev polynomials. Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:flat}(a) shows that the product is far smaller, away from the interval, than a single truncation of the same degree. It also shows that the number $k$ of factors must be chosen with care: with $k=3$ and $l=6$ the product satisfies the second condition only for $\eta\ge0.78$, not for $\eta=1/3$, and for $k$ between $3$ and $8$ the least admissible $\eta$ lay between $2.3/k$ and $3.7/k$. Part (b) compares the degrees that the two series need on $[-t,t]$: Chebyshev's advantage is a factor of about $1.7$, smaller than the ratio of the sufficient conditions $\ee t+\ln(1/\eps)$ and $10t+\ln(1/\eps)$ that are usually quoted.}

\begin{frame}\ft{Samples, time and bits}
\ona{Theorem~\ref{thm:ch06:gibbs} answers three questions, and they should be kept apart. The number of copies is a question of statistics, and the Cram\'er--Rao bound of Section~\ref{sec:ch06:fisher} limits it from below. The running time is a question of algorithms: the constraints of Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:direction} define a problem of polynomial optimisation, and its moment/SOS relaxation of fixed degree is a semidefinite programme of polynomial size. The third question is the number of bits, and it is not settled by the second. \citet{o2017sos} gave a semidefinite programme of degree two, with small coefficients, whose solutions, and indeed every constant-factor approximation of them, need exponentially many bits; \citet{raghavendra2017bit} found non-negative polynomials with proofs of degree two but no proofs of degree $\Omega(\sqrt n)$ with small coefficients. Such examples rely on equality constraints. \citet{gribling2023note} showed that if the feasible set is defined by inequalities, lies in a ball of radius $R\le2^{\mathrm{poly}(n)}$, and contains a ball of radius $r\ge2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}$, then the bound of fixed degree can be computed in polynomial time to an accuracy $2^{-\mathrm{poly}(n)}$. For the learning of Hamiltonians the containment of a ball can be tested instance by instance, but it is not known to hold in general \citep{wodecki2024learning}. We return to the algorithms in \Chap~\ref{C.AlgTomography}.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item Copies: statistics, bounded below by Cram\'er--Rao.
    \item Time: a moment/SOS relaxation of polynomial size.
    \item Bits: not implied by size \citep{o2017sos}; polynomial if the feasible set contains a ball \citep{gribling2023note}.
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\section{Finite samples in system identification}\label{sec:ch06:sysid}

\begin{frame}\ft{Learning a linear system from data}
\ona{The classical theory of system identification long gave only asymptotic guarantees, of consistency and asymptotic normality \citep{JANSSON19981507,KNUDSEN200181}. In the last decade it has acquired \emph{finite-sample} bounds, which state how large the error can be for a given number of data. For the discrete system $\vec x_{k+1}=\mathbf G\vec x_k+\vec w_k$, observed directly and driven by noise $\vec w_k$ of size $\sigma_w$, the least-squares estimate from $K$ samples satisfies, with probability $1-\delta$ \citep{simchowitz2018learning,sarkar2019near},}
\onp{Fully observed, $\vec x_{k+1}=\mathbf G\vec x_k+\vec w_k$, least squares from $K$ samples \citep{simchowitz2018learning,sarkar2019near}:}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch06:ls}
    \norm{\hat{\mathbf G}-\mathbf G}_{\infty}\;\le\;\bigO{\sigma_w\sqrt{\frac{n+\log(1/\delta)}{K\,\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf\Sigma_K)}}},
\end{equation}
\ona{where $\mathbf\Sigma_K$ is the empirical covariance of the states. The error falls like $K^{-1/2}$, and it is governed by $\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf\Sigma_K)$, which measures how well the trajectory explores every direction of the state space: this is the quantitative persistency of excitation \citep{Coulson2022quantitative}. For a system observed through $y_k=\mathbf C\vec x_k+\vec v_k$, \citet{oymak2019non} analyse the algorithm of Ho and Kalman applied to Markov parameters estimated with noise, and \citet{tsiamis2022statistical} survey these results. They also exhibit systems which are identifiable but which require a number of samples exponential in their dimension: identifiability in principle does not imply identifiability at a reasonable cost.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item Error $\propto K^{-1/2}/\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf\Sigma_K)}$: quantitative persistency of excitation \citep{Coulson2022quantitative}.
    \item Partially observed: Ho--Kalman with noisy Markov parameters \citep{oymak2019non}; identifiable $\neq$ cheaply learnable \citep{tsiamis2022statistical}.
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}\ft{Ho--Kalman on quantum data}
\ona{Quantum data differ from the classical setting in two respects. First, there is no single trajectory: every sampling time is a separate experiment, with freshly prepared copies, so that the Markov parameters are estimated directly. Second, the noise is not process noise but the noise of the shots: each entry of the data is a mean of $N$ outcomes $\pm1$, with variance at most $1/N$, independently of every other entry. This is the most favourable case of the analysis of \citet{oymak2019non}.}
\begin{propn}[Shots for Ho--Kalman on quantum data]\label{prop:ch06:hokalman}
Let $\Hankel_{rs}(0)$ be formed from $p$ observables at $r+s-1$ times, each expectation value estimated from $N$ shots. Then, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch06:hankelerror}
    \norm{\hat\Hankel-\Hankel}_2\;\le\;\sqrt{rsp}\,\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}\ln\frac{2(r+s-1)p}{\delta}} .
\end{equation}
If this is small compared with $\sigma_n(\Hankel)$, the smallest non-zero singular value of $\Hankel$, the realisation returned by Ho--Kalman differs from a similarity transform of the true one by at most a constant multiple of $\norm{\hat\Hankel-\Hankel}_2/\sigma_n(\Hankel)$, the constant depending on $n$ and on $\norm{\Hankel}/\sigma_n(\Hankel)$. Consequently a number of shots per expectation value of order $rsp\,\ln(rsp/\delta)/(\sigma_n(\Hankel)^2\eps^2)$ suffices for accuracy $\eps$.
\end{propn}
\ona{\begin{pf}
The $(r+s-1)p$ distinct entries of $\hat\Hankel-\Hankel$ are each smaller than $\sqrt{2\ln(2(r+s-1)p/\delta)/N}$ with probability $1-\delta$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:ch06:hoeffding} and the union bound. The spectral norm is at most the Frobenius norm, which is at most $\sqrt{rsp}$ times the largest entry. The perturbation bound for the realisation is that of \citet{oymak2019non}.
\end{pf}}
\ona{Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:hankelnoise}(a) shows the singular values of the Hankel matrix of our qubit as the number of shots grows. The noise floor of \Chap~\ref{C.Frequency} falls like $N^{-1/2}$, about $6.6/\sqrt N$ here, and a mode becomes visible when the floor falls below its singular value. For the preparation at $60^\circ$ the weakest mode emerges at about $130$ shots per sample; for the weakly excited preparation at $15^\circ$ its singular value is smaller, and about $700$ shots are needed. Below these numbers Ho--Kalman does not merely give poor estimates; it finds the wrong number of modes. Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:hankelnoise}(b) shows the errors of the poles returned by Ho--Kalman, which also fall like $N^{-1/2}$, the slow relaxation $-\gamma_1$ being the least well determined.}
\onp{{\small Fresh copies at every time: independent errors of size $N^{-1/2}$ on every entry of $\Hankel$.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}\ft{Noise in the Hankel matrix}
\ona{Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:hankelheat} shows the matrices of Proposition~\ref{prop:ch06:hokalman} for our qubit. The error $E=\hat\Hankel-\Hankel$ is small entry by entry, but it is a Hankel matrix like $\Hankel$ itself: the error of each sample appears along a whole anti-diagonal, which is why the union bound in the proof runs over $r+s-1$ entries only. The condition of the proposition, that $\norm{E}_2$ be small compared with $\sigma_n(\Hankel)$, is the condition that the box of the weakest mode should stay clear of the floor; at $100$ shots per sample it does not. The bound \eqref{eq:ch06:hankelerror} is about ten times the actual norm, which is the cost of passing crudely from the largest entry to the spectral norm.}

\begin{frame}\ft{Weakly excited, weakly seen, nearly merged}
\ona{The quantity $\sigma_n(\Hankel)$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:ch06:hokalman} is the quantitative form of the three conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:ch05:modal} of \Chap~\ref{C.Frequency}. There a mode was lost from the data if it was not excited, not seen, or merged with another by the sampling, and the rank of the Hankel matrix fell. Short of these, the rank is full, but the smallest singular value is small when a mode is weakly excited, weakly seen, or nearly merged, and the number of shots grows like its inverse square. Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:nearly} shows the three cases for our qubit: the singular value falls to zero as the preparation approaches the $z$-axis, as the axis of measurement approaches the $z$- or the $x$-axis, and as the sampling interval approaches $\pi/\omega$, where the two modes of the precession merge. Near each of these points the system remains identifiable, but at a cost that grows without bound. This is also the content of the observation in \citet{parvaiz2025identifiability} that the error of identification diverges at sampling rates in a low-order rational ratio and degrades continuously in their neighbourhood.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item $\sigma_n(\Hankel)\to0$ as a mode becomes unexcited, unseen, or merged; shots grow like $1/\sigma_n^2$.
    \item The three ways of losing a mode (\Chap~\ref{C.Frequency}) become a continuum of cost.
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\section{From the system matrix to the master equation}\label{sec:ch06:budget}

\begin{frame}\ft{Propagating the error into the parameters}
\ona{The identifiability results of \Chap~\ref{C.StateSpace} concern exact data. In practice the system matrix is estimated, and its error propagates into the Hamiltonian and Lindblad parameters reconstructed from it. Estimating each $x_j=\Tr[F_j\rho]$ from $S$ copies incurs a standard error of order $S^{-1/2}$, so that $\lVert\vecop{\tilde{\mathbf A}}-\vecop{\mathbf A}\rVert$ is of order $n\,S^{-1/2}$, up to the conditioning of the sampling map. The parameters are then recovered by inverting the linear map $\mathbf M$ of \eqref{eq:ch04:M}, and the accuracy depends on three things: the accuracy of $\mathbf A$, the numerical error in $\mathbf M$, and the condition number $\kappa(\mathbf M)=\norm{\mathbf M}\norm{\mathbf M^{-1}}$. Although $\mathbf M$ is built from the structure constants of the Lie algebra, these may be irrational and must be rounded, so that in fact we invert $\tilde{\mathbf M}=\mathbf M+\Delta\mathbf M$. The error of the parameter vector $\vec y=(\vec\theta,\vecop\Koss)$ then satisfies}
\onp{With $\lVert\vecop{\tilde{\mathbf A}}-\vecop{\mathbf A}\rVert$ of order $nS^{-1/2}$ from $S$ copies per expectation value \citep{Parvaiz2025}:}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch06:error_bound}
    \norm{ \vec y - \tilde{\vec{y}} }  \leq \norm{ \tilde{\mathbf{M}}^{-1} } \norm{ \vecop{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}} - \vecop{\mathbf{A}} } +   \frac{\kappa(\mathbf{M})}{\frac{\norm{ \mathbf M}}{\norm{\Delta\mathbf M }} - \kappa(\mathbf M)} \norm{\mathbf{M}^{-1} }\norm{ \vecop{\mathbf{A}} },
\end{equation}
\ona{provided that $\kappa(\mathbf{M})\lVert\Delta\mathbf{M}\rVert<\lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert$, that is, provided that the rounding error does not exceed the distance of $\mathbf{M}$ from singularity. A similar analysis applies to the reconstruction for a symmetric Kossakowski matrix, with $\mathbf{M}$ replaced by $\mathbf{R}_{1}$ or $\mathbf{R}_{3}$ and $\mathbf{A}$ by $\mathbf{A}^{(l)}$ or $\mathbf{A}^{(d)}$. With the sampling error, the precision of the reconstructed parameters is therefore of order $\kappa(\mathbf{M})\,n\,S^{-1/2}$ \citep{Parvaiz2025}. Since the reconstruction is linear, the projection of $\hat\Koss$ onto the cone of positive semidefinite matrices (\Chap~\ref{C.StateSpace}) does not increase the error.}
\onp{\begin{itemize}
    \item First term: error of $\mathbf A$ amplified by $\norm{\mathbf M^{-1}}$; second: rounding of irrational structure constants.
    \item Precision of $(\vec\theta,\Koss)$ of order $\kappa(\mathbf M)\,n\,S^{-1/2}$; the projection onto the PSD cone does not amplify it.
\end{itemize}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}\ft{The reconstruction map}
\ona{For the qubit the map $\mathbf M$ is as well conditioned as one could wish. Its singular values are $2$, in the directions of the three coefficients of the Hamiltonian and of the trace of $\Koss$, and $1$ in the remaining eight. An error of $\mathbf A$ is therefore at worst carried over unchanged, and the Hamiltonian is recovered twice as accurately as the dissipator (Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:reconstruction}(a)). The second term of \eqref{eq:ch06:error_bound} is of no practical concern here. With an error of $0.05$ in $\mathbf A$, the rounding of the structure constants begins to matter only when its relative size exceeds about $6\cdot10^{-3}$, that is, when they are kept to fewer than three figures, whereas double precision gives $10^{-16}$. In larger systems $\kappa(\mathbf M)$ need not be so small, and the bound then warns that a map near singularity amplifies both errors without limit.}

\begin{frame}\ft{The whole budget}
\ona{We can now draw up a complete, if crude, budget of shots for the identification of a master equation by the methods of \Chaps~\ref{C.StateSpace} and~\ref{C.Frequency}, with $N$ shots per expectation value, $p$ observables, and Hankel matrices with $r$ block rows and $s$ columns:}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.3em}
    \item expectation values: error $\eps_0$ of order $\sqrt{\ln(rsp/\delta)/N}$ each (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch06:hoeffding});
    \item discrete generator by Ho--Kalman: error $\eps_1$ of order $\sqrt{rsp}\,\eps_0/\sigma_n(\Hankel)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:ch06:hokalman});
    \item continuous generator by the logarithm: error $\eps_2$ of order $\kappa(\mathbf g)\,\eps_1/\tau$, with $\mathbf g$ the matrix of eigenvectors;
    \item master-equation parameters: error $\eps_3$ of order $\kappa(\mathbf M)\,\eps_2$ by \eqref{eq:ch06:error_bound}.
\end{itemize}
\ona{Every factor is a condition number of an object that we have met before: the observability and Krylov factors of the Hankel matrix (\Chaps~\ref{C.StateSpace} and~\ref{C.Frequency}), the eigenvector matrix of the generator (\Chap~\ref{C.Frequency}), and the reconstruction map (\Chap~\ref{C.StateSpace}). Writing $\kappa=\kappa(\mathbf M)\,\kappa(\mathbf g)/(\sigma_n(\Hankel)\,\tau)$, we need of order $\kappa^2\,rsp\,\ln(rsp/\delta)/\eps_3^2$ shots per expectation value: the $1/\eps^2$ of independent shots, multiplied by the square of the conditioning of the whole problem. Figure~\ref{fig:ch06:budget} evaluates the factors for our qubit: $20$, $1.72$, $9.66$ and $2$, an amplification of about $660$ in all. By the bound, a relative accuracy of $1\%$ in the parameters would need about $9\cdot10^8$ shots per expectation value, whereas a simulation reaches it with about $2\cdot10^4$. The bound is pessimistic by a large factor, since it compounds a worst case at every stage and the worst cases do not occur together; but it shows where the shots go, and which stage to improve. The $1/\eps^2$ cannot be improved with independent shots, by the Cram\'er--Rao bound; only coherent protocols that use time as a resource change the exponent, and only for the Hamiltonian part. \Chap~\ref{C.AlgTomography} turns these estimates into algorithms, and \Chap~\ref{C.Revisited} applies them to the examples of \Chap~\ref{C.Motivation}.}
\onp{$N$ of order $\kappa^2\,rsp\,\ln(rsp/\delta)/\eps^2$, with $\kappa=\kappa(\mathbf M)\kappa(\mathbf g)/(\sigma_n(\Hankel)\tau)$: the $\eps^{-2}$ of independent shots, times the square of the conditioning.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}\ft{Summary}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.3em}
    \item A shot is a coin toss: $N$ shots give an error of order $1/\sqrt N$, and Hoeffding's inequality asks for $\frac{2}{\eps^2}\ln\frac2\delta$ of them; many observables cost only a logarithm more.
    \item Fisher information measures what a shot can tell; the Cram\'er--Rao bound makes the $1/\sqrt N$ law a law of nature for independent shots; the error ellipse is the bottom of the least-squares valley.
    \item The cost of a mode is proportional to $1/\abs{r_i}^2$: weakly excited, weakly seen or nearly merged modes are identifiable but expensive.
    \item The best measurement attains the quantum Fisher information; long coherent evolution gives the Heisenberg limit $1/T$, which decoherence caps.
    \item Tomography costs $d^2/\eps^2$ to $d^3/\eps^2$ copies; classical shadows estimate many local observables at a cost independent of the number of qubits.
    \item Ho--Kalman needs $N$ of order $rsp\ln(rsp/\delta)/(\sigma_n(\Hankel)^2\eps^2)$ shots; the whole pipeline costs $\kappa^2/\eps^2$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
